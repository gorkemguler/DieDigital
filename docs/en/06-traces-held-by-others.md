# 6. Traces held by others

Deleting your own accounts is half the job. Other people posted the rest: your friends, family, employer, school, newspapers, event organisers. You can't delete these yourself, but you can ask for most of them to be taken down. Most people won't refuse a polite, clear request.

## 6.1 How to ask

- **Be short and specific:** which content (a link), what you want (removal, initials instead of your name, blurring), and why (briefly; you don't owe anyone details).
- **Personal first, formal second:** a message to a friend, an email to an organisation, and only if that fails, a formal data protection request.
- **Offer an alternative:** "If you'd rather not remove it entirely, removing my name or blurring my face would also be fine."
- **Keep a record:** when, to whom, what you wrote, what they replied.

Ready-made texts: [Appendix B](appendix-b-letter-templates.md).

## 6.2 Friends and family

- **Photos and tags:** Ask them to remove posts where you're tagged or your face is clearly visible, or to crop you out. You can remove tags yourself ([chapter 2](02-content-cleanup.md)); the photo itself is theirs.
- **Set rules for the future:** Ask them not to tag you, not to post photos of you without asking, and not to post your location ("at X with Y"). Saying this in advance works well for crowded events like weddings, birthdays and graduations.
- **Group chats:** Before leaving a group, delete personal information you shared there (addresses, photos of documents). Family groups often have photos of IDs, passports and property papers floating around.
- **Shared accounts:** A family Netflix plan, shared cloud albums, a shared Google Calendar, family location sharing, shared shopping or transport accounts. Remove yourself.
- **Platform reports:** If asking doesn't work and a photo invades your privacy, use the platform's privacy violation report forms. Facebook and Instagram review privacy reports for photos in which you're identifiable and haven't given consent.

## 6.3 Work

- **Company website:** "Our team" and "About us" pages, author pages, blog posts. If you've left, ask them to remove you; if you still work there, ask for an entry without a photo or with initials only.
- **Press releases and news:** Appointments, awards, project announcements. Ask the company to update the releases on its own site; for news sites, see 6.6.
- **Conferences and events:** Speaker pages, slide decks (SlideShare, Speaker Deck), talk recordings (YouTube). Write to the organisers.
- **Job ads and careers portals:** Past employers and companies you applied to have your CV in their HR systems: send a deletion request.
- **Email signatures, internal directories, org charts:** Internal, but they can leak. When you leave, ask HR to delete your personal data except what they're legally required to keep.
- **The company's LinkedIn posts:** For posts tagging you, write to the company's social media team.

## 6.4 School and academia

- **School websites:** Honour rolls, graduation photos, class albums, competition results, scholarship lists. Write to the school; they're particularly careful about photos taken when you were a child.
- **Alumni associations and portals:** Have your profile removed.
- **Theses:** Theses held in national or university repositories are generally not deleted, though access can sometimes be restricted for a period. Ask your university's graduate school or library.
- **Academic profiles:** Google Scholar profiles can be deleted; ORCID records can be deactivated; ResearchGate and Academia.edu accounts can be deleted. Published papers remain.
- **Course and certificate pages:** Certificate verification pages are indexed under your name; ask the platform to remove them.

## 6.5 Events, sports, clubs

- **Race results:** Marathon, cycling and triathlon results sites show your name, age, club and times for years. Ask the organiser and the results site to remove or abbreviate your name; most agree.
- **Sports federations:** Lists of licensed athletes and tournament results. Send a deletion request to the federation.
- **Associations, clubs, foundations:** Member lists, board announcements, event photos.
- **Ticketing and event sites:** Attendee lists, event photo galleries.
- **Wedding photographers, studios:** Your photos may be in their portfolios. Ask them to remove them.

## 6.6 News, interviews, podcasts

- **News sites:** Write to the site first: ask for your name to be removed or reduced to initials, or your photo blurred. For old stories that no longer have news value, many publishers are reasonable. If that fails, use search engine delisting and the legal routes ([chapter 4](04-search-engines-and-archives.md)).
- **Interviews, podcasts, YouTube videos:** Ask the publisher to take the episode down or remove your name. For videos, blurring your face is another option.
- **Court records:** Rules differ widely between countries. In some, published judgments are anonymised; in others (such as the US) court records are largely public. Ask the court registry what is possible, and deal with news coverage of cases as above.

## 6.7 Wikipedia

If there's a Wikipedia article about you and you're not a public figure, the article may not meet the notability criteria. Wikipedia's policy on biographies of living persons pays particular attention to the privacy of private individuals. Ask on the article's talk page or through Wikipedia's help/email channels for the article to be deleted or personal details removed. Don't edit the article yourself; your edits can be reverted as a conflict of interest.

## 6.8 Family tree and DNA sites

- **MyHeritage, Ancestry, FamilySearch, Geni:** Family trees built by relatives may include your name, date of birth and parents. Living people are hidden by default on most of these sites, but not always. Ask the relative who built the tree to remove or hide you; if that fails, write to the site.
- **DNA tests:** For your own DNA test, see [chapter 7](07-real-world-traces.md). If relatives have tested, you're indirectly present in their match lists; there's no way to remove that, but you can ask them to turn off relative matching.

## 6.9 What others have written about you

- **Forums and review sites:** Things others have written about you under your name. Go to the site's administrators first, citing personal data. If it's defamatory or exposes your private life, use the legal routes available where you live ([chapter 4](04-search-engines-and-archives.md), [chapter 8](08-legal-rights.md)).
- **Business reviews:** If you own or work at a business, your name may come up in reviews; use the platform's process for reporting reviews that contain personal information.
