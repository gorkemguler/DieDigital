# 0. Getting started

This guide walks you through disappearing from the internet as completely as is realistically possible. It starts with finding your accounts, cleaning up your posts and getting companies to delete your data. It then covers what other people have posted about you, the traces that tie you to the physical world, how to live without leaving new traces, and how to have whatever is left deleted automatically at a time you choose.

You don't need to know how to code. Every step can be done by hand. The `diedigital` tools in this repository only speed up the long, repetitive parts; where they help, you'll see a **With the tool:** note ([Appendix C](appendix-c-tools.md)).

The guide is written for an international audience. Where laws and services differ by country, it covers the EU and UK (GDPR), the United States and Turkey, and gives you a general method for everywhere else.

## How far do you want to disappear?

Pick your goal before you start. Each level includes the one before it.

| Level | Goal | Result | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Invisible** | Nobody searching for your name, email, phone number or photo can find you. | You keep the accounts you need, but they're private; nothing about you is public. | 3-6 weeks |
| **2. Accountless** | No accounts left except the unavoidable ones (bank, government, work, health), and companies have deleted what they held about you. | Your online life shrinks to a handful of required accounts. | 2-4 months |
| **3. Clean break** | No link left between your old digital identity and your life from now on. | New email, new number, possibly a new address; at the extreme, a name change. | 4-6 months and ongoing |

The roadmap below shows which chapters each level needs.

## Who are you hiding from?

What you do first depends on who you're hiding from:

- **Curious acquaintances, employers, strangers.** Search engines, social media and photos come first. Chapters 2, 4, 6.
- **Companies, ad networks, data brokers.** Accounts, legal deletion requests, blocking tracking. Chapters 3, 5, 8, 11.
- **A specific person** (an ex-partner, a stalker, a harasser). Your address and location matter more than anything; so do mutual friends and any device that person once had access to. **Read [chapter 12](12-stalking-harassment-abuse.md) first.** Deleting things in the wrong order can destroy evidence or tip the other person off.
- **Your future self, or after your death.** Being deleted when the time comes: [chapter 10](10-dead-mans-switch.md).

This guide is not about escaping legal obligations (taxes, courts, debts, official correspondence). Government records are not deleted, and disappearing online does not make those obligations go away.

## What cannot be deleted?

Zero trace is not possible. Accepting that up front saves you a lot of wasted effort.

- **Public records:** official gazettes and legal notices, company registers (directorships, shareholdings), court records in many countries, academic publications and theses, patents. In some countries (for example the US) voter and property records are public too.
- **Records with a legal retention period:** invoices and accounting records, bank and payment-provider customer records (anti-money-laundering rules typically require several years), telecom records. Companies can keep these despite your request, but they must delete them when the period ends and must not use them for anything else in the meantime.
- **What other people hold:** address books, screenshots, group chats, emails you sent them, photos they took. Some of it can be removed if you ask ([chapter 6](06-traces-held-by-others.md)); some of it never will be.
- **Breaches:** a leaked database can't be recalled. All you can do is make the leaked information worthless by changing passwords, phone numbers and email addresses.
- **Backups:** company backups survive for a while after your deletion request, then rotate out.

The realistic goal: **you don't come up in searches, you're deleted everywhere you know of, and where something can't be deleted, you've made a formal request and have a record of it.**

## Ground rules

1. **Stop creating new traces first.** If you keep posting and signing up while you delete, the job never ends.
2. **Write everything down.** Which account, which email, when you asked for deletion, when the waiting period ends. Don't rely on memory ([chapter 1](01-inventory-and-backup.md)).
3. **Back up first.** What you delete doesn't come back.
4. **Content first, then accounts.** Reddit, Discord, Stack Overflow and many forums keep your content when you delete your account. Once the account is gone, so is your ability to delete that content.
5. **Source first, then search engines.** A search result you get removed will come back as long as the source page still exists.
6. **Email and phone number last.** Deletion confirmations, legal replies and password resets all go there.
7. **Don't log in during waiting periods.** Most platforms cancel your deletion if you log in within 14-30 days.
8. **Go quietly.** A "leaving social media" announcement is a trace in itself, and it draws attention. Tell only the people who need to know, one to one.
9. **If someone is after you: evidence first, then safety, then deletion** ([chapter 12](12-stalking-harassment-abuse.md)).

## Sample timeline (level 2)

- **Week 1:** Stop creating traces. Build your inventory and request every data export (some take days).
- **Weeks 2-3:** Content cleanup; close the big platforms.
- **Weeks 3-6:** Smaller services, legal deletion requests (companies usually have 30 days to a month to reply, 45 days under US state laws), requests to other people.
- **Weeks 6-8:** Search engines, archives, data brokers, real-world traces.
- **Weeks 8-12:** Reminders and, where needed, complaints; email and phone number.
- **After that:** A check every three months; a dead man's switch if you want one.

## Things you can't undo

- Photos and messages: back them up first.
- Digital purchases (games, e-books, music, films) go with the account.
- Services you can't close (bank, government, healthcare): move their contact details to a new email and number before closing the old ones.
- Two-factor authentication: don't lose access to an account before you've deleted it. Keep its authenticator entries until the deletion is actually complete.
- People: friends you only know online and relationships in online communities. Decide in advance who you don't want to lose touch with, and give them another way to reach you.

## The identity verification trap

Some companies ask for an ID document before processing a deletion request. They're allowed to verify you reasonably, but they shouldn't ask for more than they need to match you to what they already hold. Sending the request from the email address registered with the account is usually enough. If you do have to send a photo of an ID:

- Black out the fields they don't need (document number, place of birth and so on).
- Add a watermark such as "Only for <company> data deletion request, <date>".

## How to use this guide

1. Read this chapter, and [chapter 12](12-stalking-harassment-abuse.md) if it applies to you.
2. Copy the [Appendix A checklist](appendix-a-checklist.md) and tick items off as you go.
3. Work through the chapters in order. Each one explains why, how and what to watch out for, and ends with a short checklist.
4. Use the ready-made texts in [Appendix B](appendix-b-letter-templates.md) whenever you have to write to someone.
5. Deletion links and menu names change over time. If a path doesn't work, search the service's help pages for "delete account".
